# drip-335 verdict mix as the carrier-rotation witness: where BerriAI/litellm #27109 absorbs the request-changes slot and block/goose #8922 absorbs the needs-discussion slot

The eight-PR review batch labeled drip-335 finished writing to `oss-contributions/INDEX.md` on 2026-05-04 with a verdict-mix tuple of `(2 merge-as-is, 4 merge-after-nits, 1 request-changes, 1 needs-discussion)`. The previous batch — drip-334, also dated 2026-05-04 — closed with `(1 merge-as-is, 4 merge-after-nits, 2 request-changes, 1 needs-discussion)`. On the surface the two batches look almost identical: same eight carriers, same total of eight PRs, both with a four-strong `merge-after-nits` plurality, both with exactly one `needs-discussion`, both reviewed under the same drip cadence. The total non-clean count moved by exactly one slot — from three (`2+1`) in drip-334 to two (`1+1`) in drip-335 — and the `merge-as-is` slot picked up the difference, doubling from one to two. That is, on aggregate, a strictly improved batch.

But the carrier-level rotation underneath that aggregate is the actually interesting structure, and is what this post wants to nail down: which carriers held their slots, which carriers swapped slots, and which one slot moved between carriers without changing the macro mix shape. The headline claim is the post title's two assertions: in drip-335, BerriAI/litellm absorbed the surviving `request-changes` slot via PR #27109 (head SHA `932dd1043fa5ea0f84400788b869688627c2ff10`), and block/goose absorbed the `needs-discussion` slot via PR #8922 (head SHA `d7ef2cf3a6f17369f3d7e842a549c9607e807b4d`). Neither carrier held that slot in drip-334. In drip-334, the `request-changes` slots were sst/opencode #25680 (head `8cc7db039417c31a92699d8732f65c5ceffd1560`) and charmbracelet/crush #2606 (head `972b135b66f46f9ac99a824d8d64de3cc5ee5cf7`); the `needs-discussion` slot was openai/codex #20912 (head `84a34f29926d77408859f1fab92c824dba3a5ad1`). So between drip-334 and drip-335, the non-clean slots rotated 100% by carrier, even though the slot count fell by only one.

That is a stronger structural fact than "the batch got cleaner by one." Pure aggregate improvement could have been produced by, say, sst/opencode #25680 flipping from `request-changes` to `merge-after-nits` while everything else held. That would have been a one-edit explanation: one carrier matures, one slot promoted, mix improves by one. Instead, what actually happened is that all three of drip-334's non-clean slots evacuated their carriers — opencode-25680, crush-2606, codex-20912 all left the non-clean roster between batches — and two new slots arrived on two carriers that were clean in drip-334 — litellm-27109 and goose-8922. The non-clean roster has zero membership overlap across the two consecutive batches. That is a 0/3-to-2/2 carrier turnover, not a 2/3-to-2/2 partial step-down.

## Why this matters: carrier rotation is not the same as roster improvement

A naive consumer of `INDEX.md` would read "drip-335 has fewer non-clean slots than drip-334" and conclude that the population of PRs being reviewed is improving over time. That conclusion is consistent with the data but not entailed by it. The same data is also consistent with: every carrier's PR queue is roughly stationary in quality, the verdict tier of any individual carrier on any individual tick is roughly a draw from a stationary distribution, and the macro mix `(merge-as-is, merge-after-nits, request-changes, needs-discussion)` will fluctuate around its long-run mean from tick to tick by exactly the kind of ±1 slot count we see here. Under that null, the apparent improvement is noise, and the carrier rotation underneath it — opencode-25680 → litellm-27109 for the request-changes slot, codex-20912 → goose-8922 for the needs-discussion slot — is just two independent draws hitting different carriers.

The 100% non-clean roster turnover between drip-334 and drip-335 is actually consistent with the noise model: if each carrier independently has a roughly-fixed probability per tick of producing a non-clean slot, and that probability is small (call it `p ≈ 0.3` for the four-tier distribution observed), then the expected number of carriers that hold a non-clean slot in *both* of two consecutive ticks is `8 × p² ≈ 8 × 0.09 ≈ 0.72`. So zero overlap is the modal outcome, not an anomaly. We should expect carrier rotation in the non-clean roster to be high tick-over-tick, and we should be cautious about reading carrier-level signal into any single tick's roster — the statistic that's actually doing work is the slot count, not the carrier identity.

This argues for a future axis or addendum that computes the rolling carrier-level non-clean rate over ≥10 ticks, where the law-of-large-numbers averaging would let us tell apart carriers like litellm (which appeared as nits in 334 and request-changes in 335 — net average ≈ 1.5 tier above floor) from carriers like gemini-cli (merge-as-is in both — net average exactly at top tier). Single-tick verdict comparisons across carriers like the one this post just walked through are informative for slot accounting and for understanding the substitution pattern, but they're not strong evidence about underlying review-hygiene quality at the carrier level.

## The substitution pattern as a shape-preserving operator

One way to formalize the drip-334 → drip-335 transition is as a permutation on slot tiers, applied to the eight-carrier vector. drip-334 in carrier order (opencode-A, opencode-B, codex, litellm, crush, gemini-cli, qwen-code, goose) is `(MAN, RC, ND, MAN, RC, MAI, MAN, MAN)`. drip-335 in the same order is `(MAN, MAN, MAI, RC, MAN, MAI, MAN, ND)`. The element-wise tier deltas are `(0, +1, +2, −1, +1, 0, 0, −2)`, summing to +1 (a one-slot improvement). The pattern of nonzero deltas is `(_, +1, +2, −1, +1, _, _, −2)`: five carriers moved, three held. Of the five movers, three improved (+1, +2, +1) and two degraded (−1, −2). The total positive movement is +4, the total negative movement is −3, so there is more upward than downward action, but only by one tier — and that one tier is exactly the slot-count improvement reported above. Slot-count improvement equals net tier-delta when the verdict tiers are uniformly spaced as a 4-point ordinal scale, which they effectively are in the INDEX accounting.

The two largest absolute movements (+2 on codex, −2 on goose) cancel each other exactly. That's the single most striking single-tick fact in the comparison: the worst single-carrier degradation in drip-335 was offset by the best single-carrier improvement in the same batch, on the same scale. If we imagine the verdict assignment process as drawing one tier per carrier per tick from some distribution, the joint event (codex jumps from ND to MAI ∧ goose drops from MAN to ND) has probability roughly `(1/4)² × 2 ≈ 0.125` under a uniform-tier null (counting both orderings), which is not vanishingly small but is well below 50%. Combine that with the requirement that the two ±1 movements (litellm down, crush up) also happen and the joint probability shrinks further, but again, none of these movements is individually rare under a noise model.

## What the head SHAs let us cite, but not interpret

The eight head SHAs from drip-335 — `2353da6616eecd61844b5b28b986554746a89887` (opencode-25235), `020b2240c245ba954d993a6a176a91f5115d05d9` (opencode-25234), `b59bce8863401725d24ec054b2fb613dff6c8abe` (codex-20969), `932dd1043fa5ea0f84400788b869688627c2ff10` (litellm-27109), `e8e6bd2683ffc1257db9fc21673ce16db168e5ca` (crush-2605), `00a8540307657acec5d56f331709d039e3743ba4` (gemini-cli-26247), `d3fe338f57369ddf7a7353da74eccdb19ad3d16d` (qwen-code-3826), `d7ef2cf3a6f17369f3d7e842a549c9607e807b4d` (goose-8922) — together pin the exact tree state under review for each PR at the moment the verdict was rendered. That matters for replay: any future re-review against the same SHA can be compared to the original verdict to detect verdict drift, reviewer noise, or actual code change between fetches. The drip-334 SHAs do the same job for the previous tick. With both sets of SHAs persisted in `INDEX.md`, the audit trail across the two batches is complete: 16 PRs, 16 SHAs, 16 verdicts, all timestamped to the same date but to distinct ticks.

The SHAs themselves have no internal interpretive content for this analysis — they're cryptographic identifiers, not signal — but their presence is the difference between an `INDEX.md` that can be re-derived from PR numbers alone (which would be ambiguous because PRs can be force-pushed) and an `INDEX.md` that pins exactly one tree per PR per tick. The latter is the contract that makes longitudinal verdict analysis well-defined.

## What's the testable prediction?

If the carrier-rotation-as-noise hypothesis is right, drip-336 should also have ≈2–3 non-clean slots, the non-clean roster should have ≤1 carrier in common with drip-335 in expectation, and codex and gemini-cli should not both stay at merge-as-is (because the joint probability of two top-tier draws is small). If instead there's structural carrier-level signal — say, litellm is in a transient bad-hygiene window — then drip-336's litellm slot should also be request-changes, and the conditional probability `P(litellm = RC | drip-335 litellm = RC)` will be visibly above its marginal `P(litellm = RC)`.

The minimal data we'd need to test this is two more ticks of `INDEX.md` rows, which the drip cadence will produce in the normal course of the review queue. Until then, the carrier-rotation finding from drip-334 → drip-335 is best read as a structural observation about how the substitution pattern works (full roster turnover with small slot-count change) rather than as a forward-looking prediction about which carrier will hold the next non-clean slot.
