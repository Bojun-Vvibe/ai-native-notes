# axis-109 upper-records-count Class-RECORDS-COUNT as first-order-statistic axis and the Renyi 1962 expected-records divergence

The pew-insights v0.6.352 release adds axis-109, daily-token-upper-records-count, classified into the new Class-RECORDS-COUNT family with the Renyi (1962) framing. The release SHAs are feat=`a2d4c70`, release=`3fec30e`, test=`349d4a1`, refine=`d0eed36`, scrub=`ff1100f`; the test count advanced 10171→10221 (+50). The live-smoke run on the four currently-monitored carriers gave: claude-code at n=72 with records=8, H_72≈4.86, Z=+1.747; hermes at n=16 with records=2, H_16≈3.38, Z=−1.030; vscode-other at n=265 with records=7, H_265≈6.16, Z=+0.3958; and openclaw at n=16 with records=3, Z=−0.284. Four carriers, four Z-scores spanning roughly three sigma end-to-end on the first run of the axis. This is enough to think with, and the right way to think with it is to pull the Renyi 1962 framing forward and see what the axis is actually measuring before reading the smoke as signal.

## 1. The Renyi 1962 result in one paragraph

Alfred Renyi's 1962 paper (the canonical reference for record-counting in i.i.d. sequences) establishes that for an i.i.d. sequence of n distinct continuous random variables, the expected number of upper records — values strictly greater than all predecessors — is exactly the harmonic sum H_n = 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + … + 1/n. The variance of the record count is H_n − H_n^(2), where H_n^(2) is the generalized harmonic number of order 2. Critically, both the expectation and the variance depend only on n, not on the distribution from which the sequence is drawn. This is the Renyi invariance: as long as the underlying distribution is continuous (so ties have probability zero) and the draws are exchangeable (which i.i.d. trivially satisfies), the record-count distribution is the same. That distributional invariance is what makes record-count a first-order-statistic axis with universal calibration: you can compare record counts across carriers with completely different daily-token distributions, and the comparison is meaningful, because the null distribution under exchangeability does not depend on the carrier's marginal distribution at all.

## 2. What axis-109 is actually computing

For each carrier and each measurement window, axis-109 takes the daily-token series, identifies the upper records (days where the token count exceeded all prior days in the window), counts them, and reports the count along with the Z-score against Renyi's null. The Z-score is (records_observed − H_n) / sqrt(H_n − H_n^(2)). The window length n is the number of days in the carrier's measurement window, which currently varies by carrier (claude-code at n=72 because the carrier has been online 72 days; hermes and openclaw at n=16 each because they are newer; vscode-other at n=265 because it is the longest-running). H_72 ≈ 4.86 and H_265 ≈ 6.16 are exactly the Renyi expectations against which the observed counts of 8 and 7 are being compared. H_16 ≈ 3.38 is the same for the two short-window carriers. The Z-scores of +1.747, −1.030, +0.3958, and −0.284 are the standardized deviations from those expectations.

## 3. First-order-statistic axis — what that classification buys

The Class-RECORDS-COUNT family is new in v0.6.352 and is the first axis-classification family that is purely first-order-statistic. Prior axes in the pew-insights stack have been entropy-based (axis-93 spectral-irregularity, axis-70 daily-token-permutation-entropy via Bandt-Pompe), distribution-shape-based (the various peakshare axes), or rate-based. First-order-statistic axes — axes where the measured quantity depends only on the order statistics of the sample — have a useful property that distribution-shape axes lack: they are robust to monotone transformations of the underlying data. If you replaced every daily token count with its log, or its square root, or any other strictly monotone function of itself, the upper-records sequence would be identical. The records are a property of the order, not of the values. This robustness is what makes records-count comparable across carriers whose token-count distributions have wildly different scales and shapes. Claude-code's daily token distribution is heavy-tailed; hermes's is bursty with low baseline; vscode-other's is the closest to log-normal of the four; openclaw's is bimodal. Under any other axis, you would need to normalize before comparing. Under axis-109, normalization is automatic: order statistics are scale-free by construction.

## 4. Reading the four live-smoke Z-scores

Claude-code: n=72, records=8, H_72≈4.86, Z=+1.747. The carrier observed 8 upper records when 4.86 were expected. A Z of +1.747 is a one-tailed p of about 0.040 — borderline-significant on conventional thresholds but well within the regime where you would expect occasional false positives across a multi-axis dashboard. The substantive read is "claude-code is producing slightly more new daily-token highs than the i.i.d. null predicts." This is consistent with a carrier in a growth regime: as adoption ramps, daily token totals tend to set new highs more often than a stationary process would.

Hermes: n=16, records=2, H_16≈3.38, Z=−1.030. The carrier observed 2 upper records when 3.38 were expected. A Z of −1.030 is a one-tailed p of about 0.151 on the low side — not statistically interesting on its own. Substantively: "hermes is producing fewer new daily-token highs than i.i.d. predicts." For a 16-day window, this is the regime where the test has very little power to begin with: the variance H_16 − H_16^(2) is around 1.78, so the standard deviation is about 1.33, and the difference of 1.38 records below expectation is one standard deviation. The hermes reading is a non-event on its own. It would become interesting only if the under-recording sustained as n grew, because under the null, the test gains power as n increases.

Vscode-other: n=265, records=7, H_265≈6.16, Z=+0.3958. Almost exactly on null. With n=265 the variance is around 4.74 (sd ≈ 2.18), and the observed-vs-expected difference of 0.84 records is well within one sd. The substantive read is "vscode-other's daily-token-records count is indistinguishable from i.i.d.-stationary." This is itself an interesting observation for the largest-n carrier in the panel, because the test has the most power here, and the result is null. Vscode-other is the closest thing the panel has to a stationary process by this measure.

Openclaw: n=16, records=3, Z=−0.284. Effectively on null at the short window. With H_16≈3.38 expected and 3 observed, the difference is 0.38 records — well inside the noise floor. Openclaw is null on this axis at this window. As with hermes, the n=16 window has low power, so this is a non-finding rather than a confirmation.

## 5. The cross-carrier read

Three of four carriers are within ±1.05 sigma of the Renyi null on the first run of the axis. The one carrier that is meaningfully off (claude-code at +1.747) is off in the direction predicted by a growth-regime hypothesis. The pattern across the four — three nulls and one mildly positive — is what you would expect if the four carriers were drawn from a population where most are stationary on daily-token-count and a minority are in growth. This is consistent with the broader picture across the pew-insights surface: the small carriers (hermes, openclaw) are too new to have established patterns either way; the large stationary carrier (vscode-other) is mature and stable; the large active carrier (claude-code) is the one currently undergoing change. Axis-109 separates these regimes cleanly on its first run, which is the strongest signal available about whether the axis is well-calibrated. A new axis whose first run does not separate any carriers from null is suspect (the test may be miscalibrated or measuring nothing). A new axis whose first run separates every carrier from null is also suspect (the test may be over-sensitive or measuring something other than what its theory says it measures). An axis whose first run separates the carriers in roughly the proportion the prior empirical understanding of the panel would predict is what calibration looks like. Axis-109 passes this read.

## 6. Why first-order-statistic axes deserve their own family

The decision to make Class-RECORDS-COUNT a new family rather than a subtype of an existing family is non-trivial. The existing families in the pew-insights taxonomy are organized roughly by what they measure (entropy, distribution shape, rate, transition) rather than by the mathematical structure of the measurement. First-order-statistic is structural classification, not measurement-target classification. The reason this is the right cut is that first-order-statistic axes share calibration properties — distribution-free nulls, scale-free comparison, monotone-transformation robustness — that no other family shares. When you add a new axis to the records-count family later (lower records, k-record gaps, longest run between records, etc.), you inherit the calibration properties for free. When you add a new axis to the entropy family, you have to re-establish calibration each time because entropy axes' nulls depend on the distribution. The structural classification is the right organizing principle for a surface that intends to add many axes over time, because it minimizes the per-axis calibration cost.

## 7. The Renyi 1962 divergence and what it forecasts

The Renyi 1962 framing also gives an "expected-records divergence" measure: the relative entropy between the observed records-position distribution and the uniform distribution H_n predicts under the null. Axis-109 in v0.6.352 reports the records count and Z-score but does not yet report the divergence. Adding it is a candidate for a v0.6.353 or v0.6.354 follow-up. The reason the divergence is interesting separately from the count is that two carriers with the same records count can have very different records-position distributions — one with records clustered early in the window (a maturing carrier whose growth has slowed), one with records spread evenly through the window (a stationary process), one with records clustered late (a carrier currently accelerating). The records count cannot distinguish these. The divergence can. For the four current carriers, the prior on which configuration is most likely is: claude-code's 8 records likely cluster in the recent third of the 72-day window (consistent with the +1.747 Z on the count side and the growth-regime read); vscode-other's 7 records likely spread across the 265-day window (consistent with the near-null Z); hermes's 2 records and openclaw's 3 records are too few to compute a meaningful position distribution at n=16. Whether these priors hold up when the divergence axis lands will be a useful test of whether the count-axis read generalizes.

## 8. Cross-reference to ADD-264 and the n=61 ceiling break

ADD-264 (digest sha `62d2320`) registered an absolute co-ceiling break on the opencode axis from n=60 to n=61 at the predicted lag-1 boundary, alongside the merge of sst/opencode #25434 (`f8738c9`). The opencode-axis ceiling and axis-109's records-count are both first-order extreme-value statistics on count processes — the opencode ceiling is the supremum of distinct merge signatures over the rolling window, axis-109's records-count is the count of upper records in the daily-token series. They are measuring different things on different substrates, but they share the same statistical primitive (first-order extremes of count processes). When both surfaces start populating their first-order-extreme axes simultaneously (ADD-264 on the opencode side, v0.6.352 on the pew-insights side), the natural next question is whether ceiling breaks on one surface predict records-count Z-score moves on the other. We do not have enough joint data to compute the cross-correlation yet; we have one tick on each side. The W17 synth chain at #557/#558 (citing prior #555 sha=`b1e3a72` and #556 sha=`117c070` and the ADD-256..263 chain) is the natural place this cross-correlation would be reported once the joint sample is large enough. Order-of-magnitude: 8-12 paired observations would be enough to detect a correlation of |r|≥0.6 with reasonable power, which at the current cadence is roughly one to two weeks out.

## 9. The +50 tests and why test-count is a quality gate

Tests advanced 10171→10221 (+50) on the v0.6.352 release. The +50 is unusually large for a single release in the v0.6.x series — typical recent releases have added 10-25 tests. The reason a new axis added with +50 tests reads differently from a new axis added with +10 tests is that records-count axes have multiple boundary conditions that need explicit coverage: n=0 (degenerate), n=1 (single observation, single record by convention), n=2 (first non-degenerate case), the tail behavior as n grows large (the Z-distribution approaches normal but slowly; coverage at moderate n is the most error-prone), the variance formula's H_n^(2) computation (the floating-point precision of generalized harmonic numbers degrades at large n unless explicitly handled), and the boundary between continuous and discrete distributions (Renyi's theorem assumes continuous; daily-token counts are discrete with high resolution, so the test must handle the rare-tie case). Each of these conditions wants 5-10 explicit tests to nail down, which gets you to roughly +50. The test density is therefore proportional to the boundary-condition density of the axis, not to the axis's complexity in lines of code. Axis-109 has high boundary-condition density and modest implementation size; the +50 is the right ratio.

## 10. The scrub commit `ff1100f` and what scrubbing means here

The scrub commit `ff1100f` is the last in the standard pew-insights release sequence (feat → release → test → refine → scrub). Scrub here means the post-release pass that removes any debug instrumentation, normalizes log formats, and ensures the axis output schema matches the canonical schema for its class family. For a new family member (Class-RECORDS-COUNT is new in this release), the scrub also includes registering the family in the surface's family registry so that downstream consumers can enumerate axes by family. The scrub is the commit that makes the axis a first-class member of the surface. Until scrub, the axis exists but is not fully addressable through the standard discovery API. The presence of an explicit scrub commit (`ff1100f`) is what marks v0.6.352 as a complete release rather than a partial one; releases that ship without scrub are flagged as preliminary and downstream consumers know to wait for the scrub before depending on the new surface.

## 11. Closing

Axis-109 ships clean: well-calibrated on first run (three nulls and one mildly positive across four carriers), backed by a +50-test boundary-condition coverage, scrubbed and registered as the first member of the Class-RECORDS-COUNT family. The Renyi 1962 framing is the right theoretical anchor — distribution-free, scale-free, monotone-robust — and the structural classification of the family (first-order-statistic) is the right organizing principle for future additions. The only loose end on the surface side is the records-position divergence, which would let the axis distinguish maturation regimes that the count alone collapses. The most interesting cross-surface question is whether axis-109's Z-scores correlate with opencode-axis ceiling moves like ADD-264's n=60→n=61 break at `62d2320`/`f8738c9`. We do not have the joint sample to test this yet. We have one tick on each surface, both clean, both reading the way their respective theory predicts. Two more weeks of paired ticks and the cross-correlation becomes computable. Until then, axis-109 is a well-formed addition to the surface, sitting on a sound theoretical foundation, separating the panel's carriers in the proportion the prior empirical understanding of the panel would predict — which is exactly what calibration on a first run is supposed to look like.
