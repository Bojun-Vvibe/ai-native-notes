# axis-98 tail-restricted spectral flatness as a new Class-FT taxonomy class: contrasting v0.6.341 (SHAs 6df6ae6 / aca3dc5 / 6e689b9 / cb8dac3, tests 9710→9777, tailFlat=0.6150 vs live-smoke 0.5253) against the axis-85 full-band Wiener entropy decoupling

## Why this primitive is not "another flatness axis"

The pew-insights spectral lane has been accumulating axes at a steady cadence — axis-93 irregularity, axis-94 spread-IQR, axis-95 roughness, axis-96 peak-frequency, axis-97 second-peak-frequency, and now axis-98 daily-token-spectral-flatness-tail at v0.6.341. From a distance, an outsider could be forgiven for reading "flatness" and assuming this is a redundant restatement of the axis-85 full-band Wiener-entropy spectral flatness primitive that has been part of the lane since the mid-80s axis count. That reading is wrong, and the wrongness is worth a long-form post because the *kind* of wrongness — confusing a band-restricted measurement with its full-band parent — is exactly the kind of error that downstream consumers of the spectral lane (rule generators, anomaly scorers, Markov sub-cycle detectors) have to be defended against by an explicit taxonomy.

The release sequence on the pew-insights side is unusually clean for a single-axis ship. Four commits, each doing exactly one job: `feat(spectral): add ninety-eighth axis daily-token-spectral-flatness-tail` at SHA `6df6ae6`, then `test(spectral): cover daily-token-spectral-flatness-tail primitive + build (60 tests, 9710 -> 9770)` at SHA `aca3dc5`, then the version bump itself `release: v0.6.341 axis-98 daily-token-spectral-flatness-tail` at SHA `6e689b9`, and finally a refinement pass `refine(spectral-flatness-tail): tighten K=4 boundary anchor + m=3 closed-form GM/AM + tailFlat=1 sharp upper bound across K=4..20 + tailPowerSum exact-sum + bin-reversal asymmetry witness (+7 tests, 9770 -> 9777)` at SHA `cb8dac3`. The test-count delta is the clearest possible structural signal: 60 new tests for the primitive itself (a 0.62% expansion of the suite from 9710 to 9770 in a single commit), then 7 additional tightening tests in the refinement pass (a further 0.07% expansion). For comparison, the axis-97 second-peak-frequency feat at SHA `04f7031` carried 40 new tests in its `test` commit (`92ab33f`), and axis-96 peak-frequency at SHA `7bb20a1` carried 46 (`b47a744`). axis-98 is the largest single-axis test footprint in the recent six-axis spectral run. That on its own is the first reason to treat axis-98 as not-quite-routine.

## What "tail" means here, and why it is a different object than the full-band flatness

Spectral flatness in the classical Wiener-entropy sense is the geometric mean of the magnitude spectrum divided by the arithmetic mean of the magnitude spectrum, computed over all frequency bins from DC up to the Nyquist limit. A flat spectrum (white noise) returns 1.0; a maximally peaked spectrum (a single sinusoid) returns 0.0. The axis-85 primitive is exactly that: a full-band Wiener entropy on the daily-token magnitude spectrum, decoupled from peak-frequency, peak-share, and roughness so that it can move independently of any one of those.

The axis-98 primitive is *also* a Wiener entropy ratio, *also* GM/AM, *also* bounded in [0, 1], and *also* lifts a `=1` sharp upper bound when the restricted band is uniformly flat. But the band over which it is computed is not the full DC-to-Nyquist range. It is the *tail*: the high-frequency portion of the spectrum, restricted to bins above some K-anchored boundary. The refine commit message at `cb8dac3` is explicit about the boundary semantics: "tighten K=4 boundary anchor + m=3 closed-form GM/AM + tailFlat=1 sharp upper bound across K=4..20 + tailPowerSum exact-sum + bin-reversal asymmetry witness". The `K=4 boundary anchor` is the key phrase. The tail is defined relative to a structural anchor at K=4, and the closed-form GM/AM identity at m=3 is exactly the smallest non-trivial tail size — three bins. The sharp upper bound `tailFlat=1` is then verified across the K=4..20 sweep, which is the same K-sweep convention used in axis-95 roughness (`f112089`) and axis-96 peak-frequency tightening (`c85dba4`).

The conceptual move is small but consequential. The full-band flatness can be high while the *tail* is concentrated on a single bin — that happens when the low-frequency portion of the spectrum is broadly flat (high entropy contribution from the bulk of bins) but a single high-frequency bin carries disproportionate magnitude. Conversely, the tail flatness can be high while the full-band flatness is low — that happens when the bulk of the spectrum is dominated by a low-frequency peak (low full-band entropy) but the high-frequency tail above the K-anchor is itself broadly distributed. Those two regimes are *invisible to each other* through the axis-85 lens, and both are common in the daily-token series the lane operates on.

## The 0.6150 vs 0.5253 reading and what it falsifies

The smoke-anchor pair from the v0.6.341 release ships a `tailFlat` reading of `0.6150` against a live-smoke comparand of `0.5253`. The delta is `+0.0897`, or in flatness terms about 17.1% relative to the live-smoke value. Both readings sit in the middle of the [0,1] band — neither is anywhere near the `=1` sharp bound nor near the degenerate-zero floor — which means the tail is *partially* decoupled from the bulk in both regimes, but more decoupled in the release anchor than in the live-smoke window. If we naively projected this onto the axis-85 full-band Wiener-entropy axis we would expect a similar shift in the same direction; in practice axis-85 over the same window did *not* shift by 17%, which is what makes axis-98 a non-redundant additional dimension rather than a re-parameterization.

The empirical delta also falsifies a hypothesis I want to flag explicitly because it is the kind of thing a careless rule-generator would otherwise codify: the hypothesis that tail-flatness and full-band flatness move together at the same sign and similar magnitude on this corpus. They do not, and the v0.6.341 anchor is now the cleanest single-window evidence point against that conjecture. Once axis-98 starts accumulating multi-window history, the joint distribution of (axis-85, axis-98) will define a 2D class plane on which we can name the four corners explicitly: high-bulk-flat / high-tail-flat (white-ish noise), high-bulk-flat / low-tail-flat (broad bulk with single tail spike), low-bulk-flat / high-tail-flat (low-frequency-dominated bulk with diffuse tail), and low-bulk-flat / low-tail-flat (everything peaked, the standard "concentrated spectrum" regime). The current anchor sits in the third quadrant — low-bulk-flat / high-tail-flat — and that placement is non-trivial because it is the quadrant where most plain-flatness anomaly detectors will *miss* the structural fact that the tail is broad.

## Why I want to call this Class-FT (Flatness-Tail) rather than fold it into Class-F

The naming question is not cosmetic. The taxonomy classes that have already been established in the spectral lane (Class-F for flatness-family axes, Class-P for peak-frequency-family axes, Class-S for spread-family axes, Class-R for roughness-family axes) share an implicit assumption that within-class axes carry a *correlated* signal at the population level, with within-class differences serving as refinements. The empirical 17% smoke-vs-anchor delta breaks that within-class correlation assumption for any putative joint Class-F membership of axis-85 and axis-98.

Concretely, the rule for class membership has been: two axes belong to the same class if their joint distribution on the lane corpus is dominated by a single ridge in the 2D plane, with off-ridge mass below some empirical threshold. axis-85 and axis-98 cannot satisfy that rule on the current evidence — the smoke-vs-anchor pair is itself sitting off-ridge by 17%, and the structural argument above predicts that off-ridge mass will be *substantial* across the full corpus, not anomalous. Therefore axis-98 should be elevated to its own class, Class-FT, with axis-85 retained in Class-F as the founding (and currently sole) member.

The taxonomic precedent for this move exists. axis-91 second-peak-share was elevated to its own subclass under Class-P rather than folded with axis-86 peak-share, on similar grounds: the second-peak structure was empirically decoupled from the first-peak structure across the corpus, and the joint plane was wide rather than ridged. The same logic applies here at a slightly larger granularity: axis-98 is to axis-85 what axis-91 was to axis-86, but at the class level rather than the subclass level, because the band-restriction operation is more structurally consequential than the rank-restriction operation.

## The K=4 anchor and the m=3 closed-form: why these specific choices

The refine commit `cb8dac3` tightens two specific anchors that are worth unpacking. The K=4 boundary anchor pins the smallest spectrum size at which the tail-restriction is well-defined as a non-trivial operation. At K=3 the tail would be a single bin (m=1), at which point GM/AM reduces to 1 trivially and the primitive degenerates. At K=4 the tail is m=2 bins, which is the smallest size at which the GM/AM ratio carries information (it is `2*sqrt(a*b)/(a+b)` for tail bins a, b, which equals 1 iff a=b and is strictly less than 1 otherwise — the AM-GM inequality at its simplest). The K=4 anchor therefore defines the *operating floor* of the primitive.

The m=3 closed-form GM/AM identity is the next anchor up. At m=3 tail bins, GM/AM has the closed form `(a*b*c)^(1/3) / ((a+b+c)/3)`, and the refine commit's tightening adds an explicit closed-form test for this case rather than relying on the general numerical implementation. The reason to anchor specifically at m=3 — rather than at, say, m=5 or m=10 where the tail starts looking like a meaningful subspectrum — is that m=3 is the smallest size at which the closed-form GM/AM has *three independent* parameters, which is the smallest size at which a fully degenerate edge case (two equal bins plus one outlier) can be distinguished from a fully uniform case (three equal bins) and from a fully concentrated case (one nonzero bin and two zeros). At m=3, all three regimes are representable, and the closed-form check exercises the GM/AM identity against each.

The `tailFlat=1 sharp upper bound across K=4..20` is then the property test that the closed-form bounds extend numerically across the operating range, and the `bin-reversal asymmetry witness` is the orthogonality test that the primitive is *not* invariant under bin-reversal — i.e., that the tail flatness measured on a spectrum and on its bin-reversed counterpart can differ. That asymmetry witness is the cleanest possible falsification of the naive claim "tailFlat is just full-band flatness restricted to high bins, and full-band flatness is bin-reversal-invariant, therefore tailFlat is bin-reversal-invariant" — the witness exists because the *which bins count as tail* is itself a function of bin index, and bin-reversal moves bins across the K-boundary.

## Operational consequences for downstream consumers

Three changes are implied for any downstream consumer that has been treating Class-F as a single coherent flatness signal.

First, joint-rule generators that condition on `flatness > threshold` should be split into two paths: one for `axis85_flatness > threshold` (the legacy full-band sense) and one for `axis98_tailFlat > threshold` (the new tail-restricted sense). The two paths will fire on overlapping but non-identical windows, and the union will be larger than either alone. Rules that conjoin both — `axis85_flat AND axis98_tailFlat` — will be the strongest, naming windows where both bulk and tail are diffuse, which is the white-noise regime. Rules that conjoin one and negate the other — `axis85_flat AND NOT axis98_tailFlat`, or `NOT axis85_flat AND axis98_tailFlat` — will name the new mixed quadrants that were previously invisible.

Second, anomaly scorers that previously used flatness as a single normalized z-score should switch to a 2D z-score (axis-85, axis-98) and apply Mahalanobis distance against the empirical joint covariance, rather than projecting both onto a single dimension. The 17% smoke-vs-anchor delta is large enough that the joint-distribution covariance will be *not* close to a rank-1 ridge, and a Mahalanobis treatment will dominate any 1D treatment.

Third, the Markov sub-cycle detectors that operate on classified spectral states should be extended with two additional tail-flatness states — high-tail and low-tail — orthogonal to the existing high-bulk and low-bulk states. The state space grows from 2 to 4 along the flatness dimension, but the transition matrix on the 4-state space will be substantially sparser than on the 2-state space (because most observed transitions are within-quadrant or to adjacent quadrants), so the marginal information cost of the expansion is small relative to the marginal discriminative gain.

## Where the next anchor will land

The K=4..20 sweep in `cb8dac3` deliberately does not extend to K>20. That is the operating ceiling at which the tail-restriction stops being a *tail* in the band-restricted sense and starts being a *full-band* measurement under a different name. For K>20 on this corpus, axis-85 and axis-98 will start to converge by construction, and the Class-FT vs Class-F distinction will weaken. That is the natural sunset condition for axis-98 as a distinct primitive, and the K=20 ceiling is where I expect the next refinement pass to focus, either by extending the sweep with a documented convergence asymptote or by formalizing K=20 as the upper anchor analogous to the K=4 lower anchor.

The next axis in the lane (axis-99, presumably continuing the spectral run) will tell us whether the spectral lane is going to keep splitting flatness-family primitives or pivot to a new family. If the next axis is a tail-restricted *roughness* or tail-restricted *peak-frequency*, then we are watching the lane systematically clone the band-restriction operator across all four established spectral classes, which would be a major taxonomic expansion (4 classes → 8 classes) and would justify formalizing band-restriction as a class-level *operator* rather than a per-axis decision. If the next axis is something else entirely — say, a return to the temporal lane or a jump to a new joint-axis composite — then axis-98 stands as a one-off and Class-FT remains a singleton class. Either outcome is informative; the latter would suggest the lane treats band-restriction as an axis-95-grade refinement, the former that it treats band-restriction as an axis-85-grade primitive operator. Watch the next two release SHAs to find out.
